The Center of Everything

Sun

The Sun is a nearly perfect sphere of hot plasma at the center of the Solar System. It provides the energy that supports life on Earth.

Inside the Sun's core, nuclear fusion turns hydrogen into helium, creating vast amounts of energy. This energy travels outward, taking thousands of years to reach the surface, then just 8 minutes to reach Earth as sunlight. The Sun's magnetic activity causes sunspots, solar flares, and the solar wind, which affects the entire solar system.

The Planets

Mercury

The smallest planet and closest to the Sun. It has a cratered surface much like Earth's Moon and experiences extreme temperature swings.

Mercury is named after the Roman messenger god because of its speed. Despite being the closest planet to the Sun, it is not the hottest; that title belongs to Venus. Mercury has a massive iron core that makes up about 85% of the planet's radius, creating a magnetic field that is about 1% as strong as Earth's.

Distance58 Million km
Size (Radius)2,440 km
FeatureShortest year (88 days)

Venus

Often called Earth's twin due to size, but with a runaway greenhouse effect making it the hottest planet in our solar system.

Venus has an atmosphere so thick that the pressure on its surface is 90 times that of Earth. Its surface is hidden by permanent clouds of sulfuric acid. A day on Venus lasts longer than its year, and it is the only planet to rotate clockwise, meaning the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east.

Distance108 Million km
Size (Radius)6,052 km
FeatureRotates backwards (Retrograde)

Earth

Our home and the only known planet to harbor life. It has a complex atmosphere and a vast global ocean covering 70% of its surface.

Earth is the only place in the universe where life has been confirmed. Its atmosphere is 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, providing the perfect balance for life. The planet's magnetic field protects us from solar radiation, and our moon stabilizes the Earth's wobble, which helps keep our climate steady.

Distance149.6 Million km
Size (Radius)6,371 km
FeatureOnly planet with liquid surface water

Mars

Known as the Red Planet due to iron oxide on its surface. It host the largest volcano and most profound canyon system in the solar system.

Mars once had liquid water on its surface, as evidenced by ancient riverbeds and lake systems. Today, water exists mostly as ice in the polar caps and beneath the surface. NASA's rovers are currently exploring the planet to see if it ever supported microbial life.

Distance228 Million km
Size (Radius)3,390 km
FeatureHome to Olympus Mons

Jupiter

The largest planet in our solar system. A gas giant made mostly of hydrogen and helium, featuring the famous centuries-old Great Red Spot storm.

Jupiter acts like a cosmic vacuum cleaner, using its massive gravity to pull in or deflect asteroids and comets that might otherwise hit the inner planets. It has a powerful magnetic field and dozens of moons, including Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system, which is even bigger than Mercury.

Distance778 Million km
Size (Radius)69,911 km
FeatureContains over 75 moons

Saturn

Adorned with a dazzling, complex system of icy rings. It's the second-largest planet and could float in water because it's so low-density.

Saturn's rings are made of billions of small chunks of ice and rock, coated with dust. While huge, the rings are remarkably thin, averaging only about 30 feet in thickness. Saturn also has at least 82 moons, including Titan, the only moon in the solar system with a thick atmosphere and liquid lakes on its surface.

Distance1.4 Billion km
Size (Radius)58,232 km
FeatureMost extensive ring system

Uranus

An 'ice giant' with a unique tilt that makes it rotate on its side. It has 13 faint rings and 27 small moons named after literary characters.

Uranus was the first planet discovered with a telescope, by William Herschel in 1781. Its blue-green color comes from methane in its atmosphere. Unlike other planets, its magnetic field is tilted 59 degrees away from its axis of rotation and is offset from the planet's center.

Distance2.9 Billion km
Size (Radius)25,362 km
FeatureRotates at an 98-degree angle

Neptune

The most distant major planet, dark, cold and whipped by supersonic winds. It was the first planet located through mathematical calculations.

Neptune is nearly 30 times farther from the Sun than Earth is. Its atmosphere consists of hydrogen, helium, and methane, which gives it its deep blue color. It has 14 known moons, the largest being Triton, which orbits the planet in the opposite direction of Neptune's rotation—a unique trait among large moons.

Distance4.5 Billion km
Size (Radius)24,622 km
FeatureHighest wind speeds in the system

Cosmic Origins

Formation of the Solar System

Our solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a dense cloud of interstellar gas and dust. This cloud collapsed, possibly due to the shockwave of a nearby exploding star, called a supernova.

When this dust cloud collapsed, it formed a solar nebula—a spinning, swirling disk of material. At the center, gravity pulled more and more material in, eventually forming the Sun. The remaining material began to clump together (accretion), forming the planets, moons, and asteroids we see today.
